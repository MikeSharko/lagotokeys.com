You don't need a grand piano to start lessons. But the instrument at home shapes how practice feels, so the question **digital piano vs acoustic piano for beginners** comes up with almost every new family. Here is what I recommend.

## Acoustic piano: the richest sound

A real upright piano gives the richest sound and teaches the fingers the most: the weight of the keys, the way soft and loud playing feels, the pedal. A good used upright is often affordable. Have it checked by a piano tuner before you buy, and plan on tuning once or twice a year.

**Choose acoustic if** you have the space, the walls aren't shared with sleeping neighbors, and you're thinking long-term.

## Digital piano: the practical choice

A digital piano is a great choice for many homes. It never needs tuning, takes less space, and has a headphone jack for quiet practice in the evening — handy in apartments and condos. Look for three things:

- **88 full-size keys**, so pieces never run out of keyboard.
- **Weighted, touch-sensitive keys** that feel heavier in the bass, like a real piano. Playing harder must sound louder.
- **A sustain pedal** that plugs in, ideally a real piano-style pedal rather than a small square switch.

A stand and a proper bench at the right height matter too: forearms should be level with the keys.

## Keyboard vs digital piano: what to avoid

Small keyboards with 61 light keys and lots of rhythm buttons are fun toys, but they build weak fingers and habits that we later have to undo. They're fine for the first few weeks while you decide — not for the first year.
